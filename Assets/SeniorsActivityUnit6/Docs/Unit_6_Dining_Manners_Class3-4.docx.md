# **1\.  The Overlap Problem, And How This Unit Solves It**

|  | Most of page 23 is what Unit 5 already taught Napkin on your lap. Elbows off. Sit straight. Do not talk with a full mouth. Ask somebody to pass things you cannot reach. Wash your hands first. All of that is Unit 5\. If Unit 6 simply repeats it, the class will be bored inside four minutes and the teacher will skip the unit next year. |
| :---- | :---- |

So this unit is built around the one thing Unit 5 could not teach:

|             You are not at home. |
| :---- |

At home, your mother knows what you like and there is nobody watching. In a restaurant there is a stranger looking after you, other families sitting close by, a menu you have to choose from, and food that has not arrived yet.

## **1.1  The difference, stated plainly**

| Unit 5 — at home | Unit 6 — eating out |
| :---- | :---- |
| Only your family is there | Strangers can see you and hear you |
| The food is already on the table | You wait, and then you choose |
| Your mother serves you | A waiter serves you — and he is a person, not a machine |
| One table | Other people’s tables all around you |
| You can leave when you like | Other people are waiting for your table |

*The only rule that repeats is posture, and here it is for a different reason: not because your mother minds, but because you are in public.*

# **3\.  Part 1 — Waiting**

Book page 26\. About five minutes. One star.

|  | Waiting is the honest hard part For an eight-year-old, the difficult bit of a restaurant is not the cutlery. It is the twenty minutes between sitting down and the food arriving. Every child in the class has been bored at a restaurant table. Starting the unit there means the class recognises the situation immediately, before a single rule has been mentioned. |
| :---- | :---- |

## **The screen**

This is the LiveTableScreen from Unit 5, reused unchanged. The difference is what sits behind Anu: two other tables with other families at them, close enough to see and hear everything.

| ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │   🙂 other table          🙂 other table                      │ │                                                              │ │      🙂         🙂         🙂         🙂                      │ │    father     mother    brother      ANU                     │ │                                                              │ │         \[ \====== empty table \====== \]                        │ │                  no food yet                                 │ │                                                              │ │              ╭──────────────────────╮                        │ │              │     STAY  SEATED     │                        │ │              ╰──────────────────────╯                        │ └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ |
| :---- |

## **The four things that happen while waiting**

| \# | What Anu starts to do | The button | If nobody taps |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | She spots a fish tank and starts to slide off her chair | STAY SEATED | She runs across the restaurant. A waiter carrying plates has to swerve around her. |
| 2 | She picks up a spoon and starts tapping the glass | PUT IT DOWN | Loud ringing. Both other tables turn and look. |
| 3 | She is about to announce how hungry she is | WAIT QUIETLY | "I am SO hungry\! Where is my food?" — very loud. Her mother looks embarrassed. |
| 4 | She kneels up on the chair with her feet underneath her | FEET ON FLOOR | She wobbles and nearly tips the chair over backwards. |

## **The other tables are the feedback**

Unit 5 used the four family faces as the score. This unit adds a second row: the two tables behind.

* When things go well, the other tables carry on with their own meals and never look over.

* When something goes wrong, heads turn. One person looks for a moment and goes back to eating.

* When several things go wrong, both tables are watching, and Anu’s mother notices them watching.

**Nothing is said and nothing is scored. Being looked at by strangers is a feeling every eight-year-old already understands, and it teaches the public-place rule far better than any sentence could.**

# **4\.  Part 2 — The Menu**

Book page 26\. About three minutes. No star of its own — it flows into Part 3\.

## **The picture menu**

A big menu opens across the board. Six dishes, each with a photograph-style picture, a simple name and a price. A child of eight can use it without being able to read much of it.

| ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │                       O U R   M E N U                        │ │                                                              │ │   \[ dosa \]      \[ idli \]      \[ noodles \]                    │ │    ₹ 60          ₹ 40          ₹ 90                          │ │                                                              │ │   \[ rice \]      \[ roti \]      \[ ice cream \]                  │ │    ₹ 80          ₹ 30          ₹ 50                          │ │                                                              │ │                                    ╭──────────────╮          │ │                                    │ CALL  WAITER │          │ │                                    ╰──────────────╯          │ └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ |
| :---- |

## **The trick that teaches the rule**

|  | If the class calls the waiter before choosing The waiter walks over, takes out his pad, and waits. Anu opens her mouth and nothing comes out. "Ummm... ummm..." She looks at the menu, then at him, then back at the menu. Her whole family waits. The waiter waits, politely. It goes on just slightly too long to be comfortable. Then the board says two words: read first. The class tries again, picks a dish, calls the waiter, and Anu orders without a pause. That is page 26’s "read the menu before you order", taught entirely by letting the class get it wrong once. No rule is ever stated. |
| :---- | :---- |

## **Then how she orders**

Once a dish is chosen and the waiter arrives, two speech bubbles appear.

| Choice | What happens |
| :---- | :---- |
| "Could I have the dosa, please?"  — correct | The waiter smiles, writes it down, says "Certainly." Sparkle. |
| "I want dosa." | He writes it down anyway and is perfectly polite, but he does not smile, and Anu’s mother gives her a small look. |

*The wrong choice is not punished. The order still arrives. Only the warmth is missing, which is exactly what happens in real life.*

# **5\.  Part 3 — The Waiter**

Book page 26\. About six minutes. One star. This is the heart of the unit.

|  | The idea Unit 5 could not teach There is a person here whose job is to help you, and he is still a person. A child who learns only that has learned the most useful thing in this unit, and it carries far beyond restaurants — to shopkeepers, drivers, cleaners, security guards and everybody else whose work is to serve. |
| :---- | :---- |

## **The five moments**

| \# | What happens | The kind way | The other way | The waiter’s face |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | He brings a jug of water | "Thank you" | Say nothing | Warm smile / polite but blank |
| 2 | Anu needs him, and he is across the room | Wait, or raise a hand | Shout and wave both arms | Comes over calmly / hurries over while other tables stare |
| 3 | He brings the wrong dish | "Sorry, I think I ordered dosa" | "This is WRONG\!" loudly | Apologises warmly, fixes it fast / apologises stiffly, goes quiet |
| 4 | He sets the plate down in front of her | A small thank you | Start eating immediately | Nods happily / no reaction at all |
| 5 | Anu drops a fork on the floor | Quietly ask for another | Shout "I dropped my fork\!" | Brings one with a smile / brings one while the room looks over |

## **Moment 3 is the one to spend time on**

|  | The wrong dish This is the first time in the entire app that a child is allowed to be genuinely unhappy about something. The food really is wrong. Anu really did order something else. The lesson is not "do not complain". It is that there is a polite way to say a thing is wrong, and it works better than the loud way — because the polite version gets the dish fixed quickly and cheerfully, while the loud version gets it fixed slowly and coldly. That is a genuinely useful skill for life, and no other unit in this book covers it. The teacher note should tell the teacher to stop and talk here. |
| :---- | :---- |

## **One small thing about the waiter character**

* He is never comic, never clumsy, and never the butt of a joke. He is calm, capable and kind.

* He is never annoyed with Anu, even when she is rude. He simply becomes less warm.

* He has a name badge, and the narrator uses his name once. Naming him is a small thing that changes how children see him.

# **6\.  Part 4 — Voices And Goodbyes**

Book page 26\. About three minutes. One star.

## **6.1  How loud is a restaurant?**

The volume slider comes back, unchanged from Unit 3\. The class already knows exactly how it works, so no explanation is needed.

Anu wants to tell her father something about school. The class sets the slider and taps SAY IT.

| Zone | What happens |
| :---- | :---- |
| WHISPER | Her father leans right across the table. "Sorry? I cannot hear you at all." |
| JUST RIGHT  — correct | He hears her easily. Nobody else in the restaurant looks up. Sparkle. |
| BIG VOICE | Every head at both other tables turns. A baby at one of them starts crying. |

*This is the third place the slider has appeared — library, playground, and now a restaurant. By now the class should be able to predict the answer, and that is exactly what learning looks like.*

## **6.2  Time to go**

The meal is finished. The restaurant is now full, and a family is standing near the door waiting for a table.

| Choice | What happens |
| :---- | :---- |
| Say thank you and leave  — correct | The family gets up. Anu waves to the waiter. The waiting family sits down and looks delighted. Sparkle. |
| Stay and keep playing at the table | Anu keeps playing with the spoons. The camera pans to the family at the door, still standing, one of them holding a small child. |
|  | **Children understand this one instantly** "No lingering in and around a busy restaurant" sounds like an adult rule with no reason behind it. Show a family standing at the door holding a tired child, and every eight-year-old in the room understands it immediately. Nothing needs to be explained. |

The third star flies in as the family leaves.

## **9.3  Build order**

* Build the menu screen first, including the order-before-reading behaviour. That single moment is the freshest idea in the unit and it should be tested with a class before anything else is made.

* Then extend LiveTableScreen with the second row of faces. It is a small change and it is what makes Part 1 feel different from Unit 5\.

* The waiter is the only significant new character. Build his three face states early and make sure the blank one reads clearly from the back of a classroom.

* Parts 2 and 4 are existing screens with new content and can be authored in a day.

## **9.4  What to watch in the first classroom test**

| Observation | What it means |
| :---- | :---- |
| The class calls the waiter before reading the menu | Almost every class will. That is the design working, not failing. |
| Children groan or squirm during the "ummm" moment | Perfect. The discomfort is the lesson and it should last about four seconds. |
| The class argues about the wrong dish | The best possible outcome. Let the teacher run with it. |
| The class predicts the slider answer before being asked | Units 3 and 6 are connecting. Exactly what should happen by the sixth unit. |
| Part 1 feels too similar to Unit 5 | Then the other tables are not visible enough. Make them bigger and closer. |
